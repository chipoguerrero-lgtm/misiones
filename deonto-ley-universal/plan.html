Lesson plan: The law everyone would sign

Deontology and ethical duty: from Kant's universal law to Levinas's face of the Other

2. Learning outcomes

By the end of the session, students will be able to:

  1. Explain a key ethical term (duty, law, universal, respect) to a partner without naming it, using description and examples.
  2. Test one of their own everyday decisions against Kant's universal-law criterion and state whether it would hold if everyone acted the same way.
  3. Write, in pairs, a definition of deontology of 20 to 26 words that integrates duty, universality and respect.
  4. Formulate, individually, a ten-rule professional decalogue written as duties and connected to their future practice.
  5. Optional Defend or revise their definition in response to Levinas's critique, during the AI examination.

3. Pedagogical rationale

The session follows constructive alignment (Biggs & Tang, 2011): each activity produces the evidence that the outcomes describe, and the rubric assesses exactly that evidence. Students reach the central concept before it is named. In the warm-up, pairs describe four key words to each other; only afterwards does the teacher combine them into Kant's idea of a law that everyone could accept. Theory is then built from questions, not from a lecture, and applied at once to situations close to students' lives and future work.

Artificial intelligence is treated as an object of critical reflection, not as a substitute for thinking. The definition and the decalogue are written without AI. The optional AI examination acts as a friction agent: it questions the students' definition but is instructed never to write it for them.

4. Teaching sequence

TimeStageTeacherStudentsEvidence / resource
0–12 minWarm-up: Describe it! Forms pairs. Partner A faces the screen; Partner B turns their back to it. Projects one word at a time (DUTY, LAW, UNIVERSAL, RESPECT) with three forbidden words. Gives 60 seconds per word and tells pairs to swap roles after each word. The partner who sees the word describes it, without saying it or the forbidden words, until the other partner guesses. They swap after every word, so each student describes two words. Slides 3–7. Number of words guessed per pair (show of hands).
12–18 minBuilding the concept Shows the four words together and asks: “What would a law that everyone would sign look like?” Collects two or three answers, then introduces Kant's universal-law formula and asks: “Would you sign a law that allows lying when it is convenient?” Answer in pairs, then share in plenary. Slides 8–9. Oral answers.
18–20 minThe critique Presents Levinas's objection with one question: “Can a rule that is good for everyone still hurt the person in front of you?” Think for 30 seconds and answer one-to-one with their partner. Slide 10.
20–28 minMission · Opening Shows the QR code for the missions panel. Asks students to open The law everyone would sign, write their names and choose their group (no student ID is requested). Choose what they would do in six situations, then name the one in which their answer would not work as a law for everyone. Slides 11–12. Answers saved on each device.
28–38 minMission · Phase 1 Monitors pairs and asks: “Which key word is doing the most work in your definition?” In pairs, write a definition of deontology of 20 to 26 words. The word counter shows how many words and key words they have used. Slide 13. Definition (in the mission).
+10 minOptional AI examination Switched on beforehand from the teacher control panel (Missions tab). Reminds pairs that the AI must not write the definition for them. Copy the prepared prompt into ChatGPT. Answer four rounds of questions, including Levinas's critique, and rewrite their definition themselves. Link to the conversation; final definition.
38–50 minMission · Phase 2 Projects the guiding questions and circulates among the students. Individually, write a ten-rule personal decalogue and the rule that would be hardest to keep. Send their work. Slide 14. Submission in the teacher's spreadsheet.
50–60 minPlenary and closing Invites three pairs to read their definitions aloud. Asks the class which rule from those decalogues they would all sign. Compare definitions and answer the exit question: “Which of your rules would you keep even if nobody were watching?” Slide 15. Oral answers; QR codes for this plan and for the missions panel.

5. Assessment

The submission is assessed with the mission's four-criterion rubric (16 points). The conversion to a final grade follows the institutional programme.

CriterionLevel 4 · Advanced (without the AI step)
DefinitionCombines duty, universality and respect in 20 to 26 words of their own, without copying Kant's quote.
Universal testIdentifies, in the opening, one of their own decisions that would not hold as a law for everyone, and explains why.
DecalogueTen actionable rules, written as duties rather than wishes, linked to their future professional practice.
Consistency and reflectionThe rules are consistent with the definition, and the student honestly recognises which one would be hardest to keep.

When the AI examination is switched on, the second and third criteria become Universal test (applies the definition to a concrete case and tells duty apart from convenience) and Critique and adjustment (responds to Levinas and justifies whether the definition changes). The mission shows the matching rubric automatically.

6. Inclusion and accessibility

  • Deaf students. The Español sencillo button changes the instructions, the six situations and the field labels into simplified Spanish. In the warm-up, the describing partner may also use gestures, signs or writing on paper or a phone. Instructions are always projected in writing.
  • Students with low vision. The High contrast button switches the mission to a black background with large text. The slides use large type and strong contrast; the teacher reads each projected word aloud only after the round, so the game is not spoiled.
  • Mixed language levels. The key words appear with short definitions in the mission, and the counter recognises them in English and in Spanish.

7. Use of artificial intelligence

The definition and the decalogue are written without AI. AI is used only in the optional examination, as a critical interlocutor that asks questions and does not write answers. Students are responsible for their final work.

8. Contingency plan

If the internet connection fails, pairs write the definition and the decalogue in their notebooks, using the guiding questions on the slides, and photograph their work to upload later. The warm-up and the concept-building stages do not depend on the network.
